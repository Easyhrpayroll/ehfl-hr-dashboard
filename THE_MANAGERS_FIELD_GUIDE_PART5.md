## Chapter 12: The Capability × Commitment Matrix — Understanding Your Team

### The Problem: Treating Everyone the Same

Most managers treat their team as a unit. "Here's what we're trying to accomplish. Everyone should do X."

But your team isn't a unit. You have four very different types of people:

- One person who's highly capable and committed (your star)
- One person who's capable but not committed (your mercenary)
- One person who's committed but not capable (your learner)
- One person who's neither capable nor committed (your problem)

Each of these needs a different approach. If you manage them all the same way, you'll fail with most of them.

### The Concept: Capability × Commitment Matrix

Capability = Can they do the job? Do they have the skills and knowledge?

Commitment = Do they want to do the job? Are they invested?

Plot these on two axes and you get four quadrants:

```
HIGH CAPABILITY
    A Players | Mercenaries
    ---------|----------
Learners    | Problem
              Employees
LOW COMMITMENT → HIGH COMMITMENT
```

### The Four Types

#### Type 1: A Players (High Capability, High Commitment)

These are your stars. They can do the job and they want to. They're invested in outcomes. They take initiative. They solve problems. They develop themselves.

**What you see:**
- Consistent high performance
- Takes ownership
- Brings ideas
- Willing to take on new challenges
- Develops others
- Stays motivated without supervision

**What they need:**
- Autonomy and trust
- Interesting work
- Clear career path
- Recognition
- To be stretched and developed further

**If you mismanage A Players:**
They leave. For a different company. They have options.

#### Type 2: Mercenaries (High Capability, Low Commitment)

These are your smart people who aren't fully invested. They can do the job. But they're not sold on the mission. They're doing it for the paycheck or the credential. They're looking out for themselves.

**What you see:**
- Strong technical performance
- Minimal discretionary effort
- Does what's asked, nothing more
- Leaves at 5:00 PM
- Focused on their next move
- Not really interested in team success

**What they need:**
- Clear value in the work (or clarity that there isn't value and they should leave)
- Challenge that matters to them personally
- Autonomy to do it their way
- Honest feedback on their next steps

**If you mismanage Mercenaries:**
They stay and do the minimum. Or they leave when something better comes along. Either way, you don't get their full capability.

#### Type 3: Learners (Low Capability, High Commitment)

These are your dedicated people who want to succeed but don't have the capability yet. They're new, or new to the role. They're trying hard. They're invested.

**What you see:**
- Effort and attitude are great
- Capability is developing
- Asks good questions
- Follows guidance
- Sometimes makes mistakes because they're learning
- Very responsive to coaching

**What they need:**
- Clear direction
- Frequent feedback and coaching
- Patience while they learn
- Scaffolding and support
- Celebration of progress

**If you mismanage Learners:**
You discourage them. They lose confidence. They leave or become problem employees. Or you micromanage them, and they never develop independence.

#### Type 4: Problem Employees (Low Capability, Low Commitment)

These are your struggling people. They can't do the job and they're not particularly motivated to learn. They're going through the motions. Or they're giving up.

**What you see:**
- Underperformance
- Low effort
- Not taking initiative
- Defensive about feedback
- Or checked out and resigned

**What they need:**
- First: clarity about the problem. "This isn't working. Here's what we need to see change."
- Second: a chance to improve (if you believe there's potential)
- Or: a respectful exit if they're not the right fit

**If you mismanage Problem Employees:**
You spend enormous energy managing them. They pull down team morale. Your A Players get frustrated watching underperformers coast. You avoid the hard conversation, so nothing changes.

### The Real Scenario: MS Dhoni and the Indian Cricket Team

Here's a famous example: MS Dhoni as team captain.

**A Players:** Virat Kohli, Rohit Sharma, Jasprit Bumrah
- Highly capable, highly committed
- Dhoni gave them autonomy and responsibility
- He delegated decision-making
- He backed them publicly
- He challenged them

**Mercenaries:** Some players who played for money but weren't fully committed to the team goal
- Dhoni gave them clear roles
- He expected professionalism but didn't worry about their personal investment
- He understood they had different priorities

**Learners:** Young players coming into the team
- Dhoni invested heavily in developing them
- He gave them specific feedback
- He put them in situations to grow
- He protected them when they failed

**Problem Employees:** Players who couldn't perform and weren't improving
- Dhoni had difficult conversations
- He made hard decisions about who played
- He wasn't sentimental about it

Dhoni's genius wasn't just as a player. It was understanding that different people needed different management. And he adjusted accordingly.

That's why he built a world-class team.

### Why Managers Get This Wrong

**Mistake 1: Treating Everyone the Same**

"Everyone should follow this process. Everyone should work these hours. Everyone should communicate this way."

But different people need different things.

Your A Player needs autonomy. Your Learner needs structure. Treating them the same frustrates both.

**Mistake 2: Over-Investing in the Wrong People**

You spend time trying to develop your Problem Employees. Meanwhile, your A Players are getting bored because they're not being developed.

Time is finite. Invest it where it creates the most impact.

**Mistake 3: Not Recognizing Mercenaries as Mercenaries**

You manage them like they're loyal and committed. But they're not. That disappointment shapes how you manage them. You become resentful.

But Mercenaries aren't bad. They're just motivated differently. Manage them accordingly.

**Mistake 4: Not Identifying Learners' Potential**

You hire someone who's low capability, high commitment. You assume they'll never get better. So you limit their opportunities.

But Learners become A Players if you develop them.

### What Good Managers Do

Good managers:
- Identify which quadrant each person is in
- Adjust their management style for each quadrant
- Invest development time in high-potential people
- Have honest conversations with underperformers
- Don't waste time trying to change people who don't want to change

Good managers also:
- Look for ways to move Learners into A Player territory (through development)
- Look for ways to increase commitment in Mercenaries (or accept they're mercenaries)
- Look for ways to exit Problem Employees respectfully

### Real Scenario: The Manager Who Used the Matrix

Arun was a branch manager. He had eight people on his team. Once he learned about the Capability × Commitment Matrix, he did an honest assessment:

**A Players:** Priya and Ashok
- Both capable and committed
- He realized he'd been treating them like the middle of the team
- He started giving them bigger projects
- He delegated more decision-making
- He developed their leadership skills

Result: Both became stronger. Ashok was promoted within 18 months.

**Mercenaries:** Rajesh
- Capable, but not fully committed to the organization's mission
- Arun stopped trying to make Rajesh love the job
- Instead, he gave Rajesh challenging technical work (which he did like)
- He paid him well for strong performance
- He understood Rajesh would probably leave someday, and that was okay

Result: Rajesh stayed two more years, performed well, and left for a better opportunity on good terms. Meanwhile, Arun stopped wasting emotional energy on him.

**Learners:** Sangeeta and Vikram
- New to the industry but highly committed
- Arun created a structured development plan for each
- He coached regularly
- He gave them stretch opportunities with guardrails
- He celebrated progress

Result: Both developed into solid B+ performers. Not A Players yet, but on track.

**Problem Employees:** Deepak
- Underperforming and not invested in improving
- Arun had the conversation: "This job isn't working. Here's what needs to change."
- Deepak didn't improve.
- Arun facilitated a respectful exit.

Result: Team morale improved. Arun could focus on developing people who wanted to develop.

By adjusting his management to match each person's quadrant, Arun's team improved significantly.

### Conversation Script: Assessing Someone's Quadrant

This is a conversation to have with yourself (or your manager):

---

**You (Reflecting):**

"Let me assess where each person is on the matrix."

**For each person:**

"Are they capable? Do they have the skills to do this job well?"

**Do they have the commitment? Are they invested in outcomes? Are they taking initiative? Are they trying to grow?**

"Based on that, which quadrant are they in?"

**Once you've placed everyone:**

"Which quadrant needs my attention most?"
- A Players: They need stretching, not fixing
- Mercenaries: Do I need to adjust my expectations or invest in engagement?
- Learners: Are they on track? What support do they need?
- Problem Employees: What happens here?

## Chapter 13: Managing the Four Types — What You Actually Do

### Now That You Know Who They Are, What Do You Do?

Knowing someone's quadrant is step one. Managing them effectively is step two.

This chapter is the playbook for each type.

### Managing A Players: Keep Them, Challenge Them, Develop Them

**The Core Dynamic:** A Players are self-motivated. They don't need you to manage them. They need you to get out of their way and give them interesting work.

**Step 1: Recognize Them**

A Players need to know they're valued. Not just in pay. In recognition.

In your team meetings, you should notice them: "I saw how you handled that customer problem. That's exactly the kind of thinking we need."

In one-on-ones: "You've consistently delivered exceptional results. And I've noticed how you approach problems systematically. That's a strength that will serve you well as you grow."

**Step 2: Give Them Autonomy**

Your job is not to tell them what to do. Your job is to set direction and get out of the way.

"Here's the outcome we need. Here's the context. I trust you to figure out how to get there. Let me know weekly, but I'm not going to direct you."

If you micromanage A Players, you'll lose them.

**Step 3: Give Them Interesting Work**

A Players get bored doing the same thing over and over. They need to learn. They need new challenges.

Stretch them:
- Give them leadership opportunities
- Involve them in strategic projects
- Ask them to mentor junior people
- Give them access to learning

"I want to give you a new challenge. I know this is outside your current role. That's intentional. I think you're ready, and I think it'll help you grow. Here's how I'll support you."

**Step 4: Develop Them**

A Players want to know: "How do I grow? Where can I go?"

Have explicit conversations about their development:
- What do they want to learn?
- What are their career goals?
- What's the path to the next level?

Then invest in that development.

**Step 5: Keep Them**

A Players have options. You need to create reasons for them to stay:
- Interesting work
- Growth opportunities
- Clear path forward
- Recognition and fair compensation
- Being trusted

If you lose an A Player, that's often your fault.

**What A Players Don't Need:**
- Micromanagement
- Constant feedback (unless they ask)
- You solving their problems
- Babysitting

**Real Scenario:** Priya

Priya is a top performer on your team. She closes 50+ loans per month. Her customer satisfaction is excellent. She takes initiative. She's solutions-focused.

If you manage her like your average performer:
- You ask for frequent check-ins
- You review her work closely
- You provide lots of direction
- You expect her to follow standard processes

What happens? Priya feels like you don't trust her. She feels stifled. She looks for a new job.

If you manage her like an A Player:
- You check in weekly (not daily), focusing on strategy and learning
- You give her autonomy to solve problems her way
- You say: "Here's the outcome. Show me your approach. I'll coach, not direct."
- You give her a bigger role: "Can you lead the new product launch? I'll support but you're driving."

What happens? Priya feels trusted and challenged. She stays and performs even better.

### Managing Mercenaries: Clarify Value, Set Boundaries, Keep Them Professionally

**The Core Dynamic:** Mercenaries are motivated by personal benefit (pay, credentials, next step), not by loyalty or mission. That's not bad—it's just different. Manage accordingly.

**Step 1: Understand Their Motivation**

Have this conversation:

"I want to understand what success looks like for you. What are you trying to get out of this role?"

Their answer might be:
- "I want to learn this skill for my next job"
- "I want to earn as much as possible"
- "I want to stay until I get a promotion"
- "I need a flexible job while I start my business"

This tells you what matters to them.

**Step 2: Clarify What You Need**

Don't expect commitment beyond the job. Expect professionalism and performance.

"Here's what I need from you: Strong technical work, meet deadlines, follow process, be a professional colleague. I understand you have other interests, and that's fine. But while you're here, this is the standard."

**Step 3: Give Them Challenging, Valuable Work**

What motivates mercenaries? Interesting work. Opportunities to build credentials or connections.

"This project will be challenging. You'll learn a lot. And it'll look great on your resume."

Mercenaries respond to work that has value for them.

**Step 4: Don't Expect Loyalty, But Maintain Professionalism**

Don't be resentful that they're not loyal. You hired them. They're honest about their motivation.

Maintain professional boundaries:
- They work for you, not with you
- You appreciate their contribution, even if they don't care about the mission
- When they leave, you support the transition professionally

**Step 5: Plan for Their Exit**

Mercenaries will eventually leave. That's okay. Plan for it:
- Cross-train someone to take their work
- Document what they do
- Build a succession plan
- When they leave, it's not a crisis

**What Mercenaries Don't Need:**
- Mission talks ("This work makes a difference")
- Emotional connection to the team
- Promises of loyalty in return for their loyalty
- Constant recognition (they're here for the work/pay, not for how you make them feel)

**Real Scenario:** Rajesh

Rajesh is on your team. He's technically strong. But he's not emotionally invested. He leaves at 5:00 PM. He doesn't volunteer for extra projects. He does exactly what's asked and no more.

If you manage him like an A Player:
- You ask him to mentor juniors
- You expect him to go above and beyond
- You feel hurt when he's not "committed to the team"
- You become resentful

What happens? Rajesh feels like you're asking for more than you agreed to. He starts looking for a job where expectations are clearer.

If you manage him like a Mercenary:
- You give him challenging technical work he cares about
- You don't expect him to mentor (that's not his priority)
- You expect solid professionalism and delivery
- You understand he'll likely leave eventually
- You have succession planning in place

What happens? Rajesh delivers strong work. He stays longer because expectations are clear. When he leaves, you're prepared.

### Managing Learners: Support Them, Develop Them, Don't Overwhelm Them

**The Core Dynamic:** Learners have the attitude but not the skills. They need structure, support, and clear feedback. They're also moldable—they'll adopt the values and practices you model.

**Step 1: Be Clear on Expectations**

Learners need structure. Don't assume they know.

"Here's what I need from you: [specific outcomes]. You probably can't hit this perfectly yet. That's okay. Here's what good looks like, and we'll build toward it."

**Step 2: Provide Regular Feedback and Coaching**

Check in frequently (weekly or bi-weekly).

"Here's what I'm seeing you do well: [specific]. Here's where I want to help you improve: [specific]. Here's how we'll work on it together: [specific]."

Coaching means: Show them, work alongside them, let them try, give feedback.

Not: Tell them once and expect them to do it.

**Step 3: Celebrate Progress**

Learners need to feel they're improving.

"Last week you struggled with objection handling. This week I saw you actually listen to the customer's concern before responding. That's growth. Keep going."

**Step 4: Give Them a Path**

Show them what's possible:

"You're new right now. In three months, I want you to be able to close a loan independently. In six months, I want you to be at 30 loans per month. By a year, you should be at 40. Here's how we get there."

**Step 5: Stretch Them, But Don't Overwhelm**

Give them challenges that are just beyond their current capability.

"This account is complex. You'll probably need help. I'll be here to coach. But I want you to lead it. That's how you learn."

Not: "Here, handle this whole process independently right away."

**What Learners Don't Need:**
- Micromanagement (they want to learn to be independent)
- Constant criticism (they're trying)
- Unfair expectations (they're new)
- Abandonment (they need support)

**Real Scenario:** Sangeeta

Sangeeta is new. She's smart. She's committed. But she doesn't have experience in home finance yet. You know she could be great with development.

If you manage her like an average performer:
- You give her accounts and expect her to handle them
- You're frustrated when she asks questions
- You assume she should know more than she does
- You don't check in regularly

What happens? Sangeeta gets discouraged. She makes mistakes because she's not trained well. She either shapes up or leaves (if she finds something easier).

If you manage her like a Learner:
- You give her clear structure: "Here's the process. Let me show you."
- You pair her with your best performer to learn alongside
- You check in twice a week: "How's it going? What are you learning? Where do you need help?"
- You celebrate small wins: "See how you handled that customer? You're learning."
- You have a development plan: "Month 1 is observation. Month 2 is paired selling. Month 3 is independent with coaching."

What happens? Sangeeta learns fast. She becomes capable. She stays because she feels invested in and developed.

### Managing Problem Employees: Have the Conversation, Give Them a Chance, or Exit Them

**The Core Dynamic:** Problem employees aren't performing and aren't particularly trying. This is the hardest quadrant to manage. You need to be clear and compassionate.

**Step 1: Acknowledge the Reality**

Don't pretend this is working. Have the conversation.

"I want to talk about what I'm observing. Your performance hasn't met expectations. I see [specific examples]. This needs to change. I want to understand what's happening. Is this a fit issue? A capability issue? A motivation issue?"

**Step 2: Understand the Root Cause**

There might be reasons:
- "I'm not getting it. I need more training."
- "My personal life is a mess right now."
- "I don't think this is for me."
- "I didn't understand what the job required."

Each has a different solution.

**Step 3: Make a Decision**

Based on the conversation, you have options:

**Option A: Invest in Development**

If it's capability + willingness to learn:
"I think you can succeed in this role. Here's what we're going to do. [Specific development plan]. We'll check in weekly. In 60 days, here's what I expect to see improve. If we don't see that, we'll need to have a different conversation."

Then actually provide the support.

**Option B: Move Them**

If they're not a fit for this role but capable of another:
"I think you're in the wrong role. You're smart, but this role isn't playing to your strengths. Let's find something that does."

**Option C: Exit Them**

If they're not capable and not willing to improve:
"This job isn't working. I've given you feedback. I've provided support. But we're not seeing improvement. I think it's time we part ways. Here's what we'll do to make this respectful and smooth."

**Step 4: Follow Through**

Whatever you decide, follow through.

If development: Coach regularly. Check in. Push them (and yourself).

If moved: Support the transition. Make sure it's a real fit, not just moving the problem.

If exited: Do it professionally. Provide severance. Allow them to save face.

**What Problem Employees Don't Need:**
- False hope ("It'll be okay")
- Ambiguity ("I hope you improve")
- Avoidance (avoiding the conversation makes it worse)
- Blame (focusing on what they're doing wrong vs. what needs to happen)

**Real Scenario:** Deepak

Deepak was hired as a relationship manager. Six months in, he's at 50% of target. He's defensive about feedback. He seems disengaged.

If you manage him like nothing's wrong:
- You hope he improves
- You complain to others about him
- You avoid giving him feedback
- Team morale suffers because mediocre performance is tolerated

What happens? Nothing changes. Deepak stays underperforming. Your A Players get frustrated. You burn out.

If you manage him directly:
- You have the conversation: "This isn't working. Help me understand why."
- Deepak says: "I'm not sure this is for me. I've been struggling since month two."
- You say: "Okay. I have two options for you. One: We create a development plan. You commit to improving. We get you to 80%+ in 60 days. Two: We part ways respectfully. What do you want?"
- Deepak chooses to leave (which he probably wanted to anyway).
- You exit him professionally.

What happens? The team can focus on people who want to be there. You can invest your energy in developing your A Players and Learners.

### Quick Decision Guide: Managing Each Quadrant

| Type | What They Need | What To Do | What To Avoid |
|------|---------------|-----------|--------------|
| **A Player** | Autonomy, Challenge, Growth | Give autonomy, stretch them, develop leadership | Micromanage, bore them, lose them |
| **Mercenary** | Clear role, Fair pay, Interesting work | Clarify expectations, give valuable work, plan for exit | Expect loyalty, feel resentful |
| **Learner** | Structure, Coaching, Support | Coach regularly, celebrate progress, have clear path | Overwhelm, criticize, abandon |
| **Problem** | Clear conversation, Real options | Have the talk, commit to development or exit | Ignore, give false hope, avoid |

### Conversation Script: The Real Management Conversation

For each person, you should be able to have this conversation:

---

**You (after assessing their quadrant):**

**For A Player:** "You're doing exceptional work. I want to talk about how I can develop you further. What interests you? What are your goals? How can I support your growth?"

**For Mercenary:** "I appreciate your strong technical work. I understand you have other ambitions. What matters most to you here? How can I give you work that's valuable for you? Let's be clear on expectations so we both win."

**For Learner:** "You're doing well. You're learning. I want to make sure I'm supporting you the right way. What do you need from me? What's hard right now? Here's my plan for developing you."

**For Problem Employee:** "I want to talk about what I'm observing. [Be specific about performance gap]. I want to understand what's happening. Help me understand. What do you think is going on? What do you need? What are you thinking?"

## Summary: Part 5 — Know Your People

The Capability × Commitment Matrix is one of the most powerful tools for understanding your team.

Not everyone needs the same management. Your A Player needs autonomy. Your Learner needs structure. Your Mercenary needs clarity about what matters to them. Your Problem Employee needs an honest conversation.

When you match your management to each person's quadrant, everything improves:
- A Players become more engaged and stay
- Mercenaries deliver strong work within clear boundaries
- Learners develop into capable performers
- Problem Employees either improve or exit cleanly

This is the foundation for everything that comes after: development, accountability, performance management.

Know your people. Manage them accordingly.
