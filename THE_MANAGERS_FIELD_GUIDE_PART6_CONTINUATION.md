## Chapter 17: How to Give Feedback Effectively

### The Three Types of Feedback Conversations

Not all feedback conversations are the same. The approach depends on the situation.

#### Type 1: Positive Reinforcement (What You Did Well)

**When:** Immediately after you see good behavior
**Why:** Reinforces the behavior you want more of
**How:** Quick, specific, genuine

"That's exactly how I want you to handle objections. You listened fully, asked clarifying questions, then proposed a solution. That's the model."

Keep it brief. Be specific. Don't overdo it (it loses meaning).

**Frequency:** Often. Daily, ideally.

#### Type 2: Coaching on Gaps (Here's How to Improve)

**When:** When you notice someone isn't doing something as well as they could
**Why:** Gives them specific information to improve
**How:** Calm, collaborative, focused on learning

"I want to help you with something. In that presentation, your numbers were solid but your narrative didn't connect the dots. Your audience couldn't see the story. Here's what I mean... Here's how you might approach it differently."

Frame it as helping, not criticizing.

**Frequency:** Weekly or bi-weekly, depending on development needs.

#### Type 3: Accountability (This Isn't Acceptable)

**When:** When behavior crosses the line or performance is unacceptable
**Why:** Clarity about standards and consequences
**How:** Direct, clear, but not harsh

"You missed the deadline without communicating why. We agreed on this deadline. This affects other people's work. Here's what needs to happen: You communicate early if deadlines are at risk. You make commitments you can keep. This matters."

Be clear about impact. Be clear about expectations going forward.

**Frequency:** As needed. Ideally not frequent—if it is, you have a bigger problem.

### The Feedback Rhythm

Best practice: Mix all three types.

**Every day:** Catch people doing things right. Quick reinforcement.

**Weekly or bi-weekly:** Coaching conversations on specific areas of development.

**Monthly or as needed:** Accountability conversations when standards aren't being met.

This creates a rhythm where:
- People know what's working (reinforcement)
- People know how to improve (coaching)
- People know where the line is (accountability)

### Feedback in High-Pressure Environments

In NBFC environments, there's often pressure and stress. Feedback can feel like criticism.

Use this framework:

**Step 1: Acknowledge the pressure**
"I know this is a high-pressure environment. Numbers matter. Customers matter. Everything feels urgent."

**Step 2: Separate person from performance**
"That's the context. And I still want to give you feedback on [specific behavior]. Not because you're bad at your job. But because I think you can be even better."

**Step 3: Be specific and kind**
"When you're under pressure, you sometimes skip the verification step. I get why—you're trying to move fast. But it creates rework later. Here's what helps: [specific approach]."

**Step 4: Reinforce capability**
"You're capable of doing this well. I see it in other calls. I just want to see it more consistently."

This framework maintains respect while still addressing gaps.

### The Feedback Conversation Checklist

Before you give feedback:

- [ ] Is this the right timing? (Not in anger, not in front of others unless it's positive)
- [ ] Am I clear on the specific behavior? (Not vague)
- [ ] Am I clear on the impact? (Why does this matter?)
- [ ] Do I have the facts right? (Or am I making assumptions?)
- [ ] Is my tone right? (Kind but clear, not harsh)
- [ ] Am I ready to help? (Or just criticize?)

## Chapter 18: Difficult Conversations That Matter

### The Conversations Managers Avoid

Most managers avoid three conversations:

1. **"You're not performing to standard and we need to fix it."**
2. **"You're not going to get the promotion you want."**
3. **"I'm going to have to let you go."**

These conversations are hard. So managers avoid them. And problems compound.

### The Concept: Difficult Conversations Are Necessary

There's a reason they're difficult: They involve honesty, disappointment, and sometimes pain.

But avoiding them creates bigger problems:
- Underperformance gets worse
- Team morale suffers (everyone knows there's a problem)
- You burn out managing around the issue
- The person's future gets worse (they're not improving, just stuck)

Good managers have difficult conversations. Not because they like them. But because they care.

### Conversation 1: Performance Accountability

This is when someone isn't meeting standards.

**Setup:** Private, scheduled conversation. "I want to talk about performance. Now's a good time?"

**The Conversation:**

"I want to be direct about what I'm observing. Your collection rate is 60%. We agreed on 75%. [Specific examples]. This is a problem. I want to understand: What's going on?"

Listen. Actually listen.

"Okay. So [summarize]. Here's what I need to happen: [Specific improvement]. Here's the timeline: [Specific date]. Here's how I'll support you: [Specific support]. Do you have what you need?"

"If we don't see improvement by [date], we're going to need to have a different conversation. Are we clear?"

**Key:** Be kind but clear. Show you want them to succeed. But be clear about expectations and timeline.

### Conversation 2: The Promotion Conversation

This is when someone wants a promotion they're not getting.

**The Setup:** This should come before they ask, ideally. Regular conversations about development and progression.

**The Conversation (If They're Not Ready):**

"I know you're interested in [role]. I want to talk about that. I think you have potential. And I also think there are some areas where you're not quite ready yet."

"Here's what I see: [Strengths]. Here's where I want to see you develop more: [Specific gaps]."

"If you work on these three things, I think you'll be in position for [role] in 12-18 months. Here's how I'll help you develop."

This is honest but hopeful. You're saying "not now" but "yes, if you develop."

**The Conversation (If They're Not Going to Be Promoted in This Company):**

"I want to be honest about something. I don't think [role] is going to open up for you in the timeframe you're hoping. And I also don't think [different role] is a good fit for you."

"I think you're capable of that job, but I also know you'd be frustrated here. Have you thought about what you might do differently?"

Sometimes the kindest thing is to help someone see they might be better served elsewhere.

### Conversation 3: The Exit Conversation

This is the hardest. You've decided the relationship isn't working.

**The Setup:** Have thought through exactly what you're going to say. Have logistics planned (severance, exit timeline, recommendations).

**The Conversation:**

"I want to talk about your role here. I've given this a lot of thought. I think it's best if we part ways."

"I know this is hard to hear. Here's why I've come to this decision: [Specific reasons, not personal attacks]."

"Here's what we're going to do: [Severance, timeline, support]. My goal is to make this as respectful as possible."

"I want to thank you for [genuine things they contributed]. I wish you well in your next role."

**Key:** Be direct. Don't soften it with false hope. Show respect even as you're ending the relationship.

### Real Scenario: The Performance Conversation That Worked

Sanjeev was a manager. He had Deepak on his team who wasn't performing. Collection rate was 55% when the target was 75%.

Sanjeev had been managing around it. Not saying anything. Just frustrated.

Finally, he realized: This is unfair to Deepak. He doesn't know he's underperforming. Or if he does, he doesn't know what to do about it.

So Sanjeev had the conversation.

"Deepak, I want to talk about collection rates. Yours are at 55%. Target is 75%. That's a significant gap. Help me understand what's happening."

Deepak: "I don't know. I'm trying. Customers just aren't cooperating."

Sanjeev: "I hear that customers are tough. And I'm also noticing that your approach might be part of it. You tend to be pretty direct with customers, especially when they're resistant. I see that leading to them shutting down. Is that fair?"

Deepak: "Maybe. I just want to get to the truth."

Sanjeev: "I get it. And I also think there's a different approach that gets better results. Instead of being direct about their problem, what if you first tried to understand their situation? Then propose solutions? I think you'd see better results."

Deepak: "I can try that."

Sanjeev: "Good. Here's what we're going to do. I'm going to listen to your calls twice a week. We'll debrief. I want to see your rate move toward 70% in the next 60 days. Can you do that?"

Deepak: "Yeah. I'll try."

Sanjeev: "I believe you can do this. I've seen you close tough customers when you're not frustrated. This is about adjusting your approach. I'm here to help."

Two months later: Deepak was at 72%. Not perfect, but moving in the right direction. And importantly, Deepak felt like Sanjeev believed in him and was helping him improve, not just criticizing him.

### Conversation Script: The Difficult Conversation Framework

---

**You:** "I want to talk about something important. Is now a good time? We'll need 20-30 minutes."

**[Person agrees]**

**You:** "I want to be direct. [The issue]."

**[Give specific examples. Not vague. Be clear.]**

**You:** "I want to understand your perspective. What's your take on this?"

**[Listen. Actually listen.]**

**You:** "Here's what I hear: [Summarize]. Is that fair?"

**[They confirm or correct]**

**You:** "Okay. Here's what I need to happen: [Specific improvement]. By [Date]. Here's how I'll support: [Specific support]."

**You:** "Do you have what you need? Are you clear on what success looks like?"

**[They confirm]**

**You:** "I want this to work out. I believe you can do this. Let's make it happen."

**[Follow up regularly]**

## Summary: Part 6 — Develop People

Development happens through:
- **Delegation:** Giving work to people so they learn (stopping doing it yourself)
- **Failure:** Allowing people to try, fail, and learn
- **Feedback:** Giving specific, kind, frequent feedback on what's working and what to improve
- **Difficult Conversations:** Being honest about performance, promotion, and fit

Each of these is uncomfortable for most managers. But they're also non-negotiable for developing your team.

The best managers are uncomfortable regularly. They have feedback conversations. They have accountability conversations. They let people fail. They delegate work they're good at doing.

That's how they build teams that get better.
