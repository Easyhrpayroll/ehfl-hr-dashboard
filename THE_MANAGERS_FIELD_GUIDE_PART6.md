## Chapter 14: Stop Doing Everything — The First Step to Development

### The Trap

You know how to do the job. You're good at it. So when something needs to be done, your instinct is to do it.

This feels productive. You get the work done. Quality is high.

But here's what you're actually doing: You're preventing your team from learning.

The work that should be a development opportunity for someone on your team is work you're doing yourself.

This is the biggest barrier to team development.

### The Concept: Doing Prevents Development

There's a direct trade-off:

**Time you spend doing work = Time you don't spend developing people**

And doing work makes you feel productive. Developing people is slower and less immediately satisfying.

So most managers choose doing.

Result: Your team never develops. They stay dependent on you. You get more and more overloaded. You burn out.

### The Math of Doing vs. Developing

Let's say you have a task that takes you 1 hour to do.

Your team member would take 4 hours to do it (because they're learning).

**Option 1: You do it**
- Task takes 1 hour
- Team member learns nothing
- You're overloaded
- Total: 1 hour, zero learning

**Option 2: You develop them**
- Week 1: You explain and they do it together (3 hours)
- Week 2: They do it, you review (2 hours)
- Week 3: They do it independently (1 hour)
- Total: 6 hours over three weeks, full learning

**But here's what happens after:**
- Week 4 and beyond: They do it in 1 hour, you're done

So developing takes more time upfront. But over time, it saves massive amounts.

More importantly, your team becomes capable. They don't need you. Your job becomes possible.

### Why Managers Don't Stop Doing

**Reason 1: It Feels Good**

Doing work makes you feel productive. You see the output. You get immediate satisfaction.

Developing is invisible and slow. You don't see the result for months.

**Reason 2: Quality Concerns**

"If I have someone else do it, it won't be as good. I need to do it myself."

True in the short term. False in the long term. Your team member will eventually be as good as you. But only if you let them practice.

**Reason 3: Time Scarcity**

"I'm too busy. I don't have time to develop anyone. I just need to get the work done."

Translation: I don't have time for the work that actually makes my job possible.

You do have time. You're just spending it doing instead of developing.

**Reason 4: Fear**

What if I give this to someone and they fail? What if they mess it up?

They probably will. That's called learning.

### What Good Managers Do

Good managers make a deliberate decision: "I'm going to step back from the work I do best and spend that time developing my team."

This is hard. It means:
- Letting someone else do it, imperfectly
- Spending time coaching instead of doing
- Having patience while they learn
- Trusting them to get better

**Step 1: Identify Work to Hand Off**

Look at your calendar. What are you doing that someone else could do?

Don't hand off your entire job. Hand off specific tasks or projects.

"I spend three hours per week on activity reporting. That should be someone else's job."

"I handle all the difficult customer conversations. Some of these could be handled by my team if I coached them."

**Step 2: Choose the Right Person**

Don't hand off your most complex work to your least experienced person.

Match the work to the person:
- Give A Players bigger challenges to stretch them
- Give Learners things they're ready to learn
- Give Mercenaries things that matter to them
- Don't overwhelm anyone

**Step 3: Set Them Up for Success**

"I want to give you this project. It's bigger than what you're currently doing. That's intentional. I think you're ready. Here's how I'll support you."

Explain:
- What the project is
- Why it matters
- What good looks like
- What success means
- How they'll be supported
- When they'll check in

**Step 4: Scaffold Their Learning**

First time: You do it together.
Second time: They lead, you support.
Third time: They do it, you review.
Fourth time: They own it, minimal oversight.

Each step builds capability.

**Step 5: Let Them Struggle (Within Reason)**

They won't do it as well as you would, initially.

That's okay. Let them struggle (but don't abandon them).

"I know this is hard. That's intentional. You're learning. Tell me what's hard and where you need support."

**Step 6: Celebrate the Handoff**

Once they're doing it independently:

"You took over activity reporting last month. You've done a great job. You're now completely owning this. That frees me up to focus on strategy. Thank you for taking this on."

Make it feel like a win, not like you're dumping work.

### Real Scenario: The Manager Who Let Go

Vikram was an operations manager at an NBFC. He was skilled at process design. So he owned all the reporting and analytics.

He was working 55 hours a week. Exhausted. And his team wasn't developing.

His boss gave him feedback: "You need to delegate more. Your team isn't learning."

Vikram's instinct: "But no one else can do this."

But then he realized: No one else can do this *yet*. But they could if I taught them.

He picked Anita, a mid-level person on his team with strong analytical skills.

He said: "I want to develop you. I'm going to teach you how I do the monthly reporting. It'll take three months. After that, you own it."

**Month 1:** Anita shadowed Vikram. Watched how he pulled data, structured the report, wrote the narrative.

**Month 2:** Anita did it. Vikram reviewed. Anita made mistakes. Vikram pointed them out. They debugged together.

**Month 3:** Anita did it independently. Vikram reviewed at a high level.

By month four, Vikram handed off reporting completely. He reviewed once a month to make sure quality was maintained.

What happened?
- Vikram's hours dropped to 45/week
- Anita developed a valuable skill
- Reporting actually improved (Anita had ideas for better structure)
- The team saw that taking on responsibility leads to growth
- Anita got promoted within 18 months because of this capability

This all happened because Vikram stopped doing and started developing.

### Conversation Script: Handing Off Work

---

**You:** "I've been doing [task] for a while now. I want to develop someone to take this over. I'd like to develop you for this."

**Person:** "I've never done this before."

**You:** "I know. That's okay. Here's what it involves: [Explain the task]. Here's why it matters: [Explain impact]. I'm going to teach you step by step. First, we'll do it together. Then you'll lead and I'll support. Then you'll own it."

**Person:** "What if I mess up?"

**You:** "Then we'll figure it out together and learn. That's the point. I'd rather you learn on something important than stay doing the things you already know."

**Person:** "Okay. I'm in."

**You:** "Great. Let's start this week."

## Chapter 15: Allow Failure and Learning

### The Safety Paradox

Here's what most managers don't realize: People learn through failure.

But most managers punish failure.

So people stop trying. They stick to what they know. They don't take risks. They don't grow.

This is the safety paradox: By trying to prevent failure, you prevent learning.

### The Concept: Failure Is Information, Not Punishment

When someone tries something and it doesn't work, that's not a reason to punish them.

That's an opportunity to learn.

**Failure is information:**
- "What did you try?"
- "What happened?"
- "What did you learn?"
- "What would you do differently?"

Managed this way, failure accelerates learning.

Mismanaged (with blame and punishment), it stops learning dead.

### Why Managers Punish Failure

**Reason 1: Fear**

"If I let people fail, something will go wrong. Then I'm responsible."

Yes. You are. But failure at low scale (a customer complaint, a process mistake) prevents disaster at high scale (your team is incompetent).

**Reason 2: Perfectionism**

"Mistakes shouldn't happen. People should do it right the first time."

In the real world, people don't. They learn by doing, making mistakes, adjusting, trying again.

**Reason 3: Control**

"If I let people experiment, I can't control the outcomes."

Correct. But if you need to control everything, you're micromanaging. And your team doesn't develop.

**Reason 4: Results Pressure**

"We have quarterly targets. We can't afford failures. Everyone needs to execute perfectly."

Translation: I'm so focused on short-term results that I'm sacrificing long-term capability.

This is how you end up running hard just to stay in place.

### What Good Managers Do

Good managers create what's called "psychological safety." This is where people feel safe trying things, failing, and learning.

How do you create it?

**Step 1: Explicitly Say Failure is Okay**

"I want you to try new things. You'll probably fail sometimes. That's how we learn. As long as you're learning from the failure and adjusting, failure is fine."

**Step 2: Respond to Failure with Curiosity, Not Anger**

When something doesn't work:

"Tell me what happened."

Listen. Don't interrupt or judge.

"What did you learn?"

Make it a learning conversation, not a punishment conversation.

**Step 3: Distinguish Between Types of Failure**

Not all failures are equal.

**Type 1: Learning Failure**
You tried something new. It didn't work. But you learned.

"You tried a new sales approach. It didn't close the deal. But you learned something about how customers respond. That's valuable."

Response: Curiosity. "What did you learn? How will you adjust?"

**Type 2: Process Failure**
You didn't follow the process and something went wrong.

"You skipped the verification step and the application had errors."

Response: "Why did you skip it? Is the process unclear? Do we need to change it? Or do you need to commit to following it?"

**Type 3: Negligence**
You didn't care enough to do it right.

"You submitted an application with incomplete information. You didn't check."

Response: "This needs to change. This isn't acceptable. Here's what needs to happen."

**Type 1 failures should be encouraged. Type 2 failures should be addressed systematically. Type 3 failures should have consequences.**

Most managers treat all failures as Type 3. That's wrong.

### Real Scenario: The Manager Who Let People Fail

Arun was a branch manager. His predecessor had been a perfectionist. "Everything needs to be perfect. No mistakes."

Result: His team was risk-averse. They did only what they knew would work. Innovation was zero. Capability development was minimal.

Arun changed the message: "I want you to try new approaches. It's okay if they don't work. What matters is that you learn."

At first, his team was skeptical. But then Priya tried a new customer intake process. It didn't work as well as the old one. So she went back.

Arun's response: "What did you learn?"

"I learned that the old process is better for this type of customer. But for another type, my approach might work."

Arun: "Good. Keep exploring. Let me know what you discover."

Over time, his team became more experimental. They tried things. Some worked, some didn't. But they were learning.

And because they were learning, capability improved.

Within a year, his team had higher engagement, higher customer satisfaction, and higher sales. Not because Arun pushed harder. But because his team was learning and improving.

### Conversation Script: The Failure Debrief

When something goes wrong:

---

**You:** "I heard about what happened with [situation]. Tell me what happened."

**Person:** [Explains]

**You:** "Okay. I hear you. So [summarize]. Is that right?"

**Person:** "Yes."

**You:** "Alright. So what did you learn? If you could do it again, what would you do differently?"

**Person:** [Reflects]

**You:** "That makes sense. I think that's a good learning. Here's what I also notice: [your observation, if different]. Does that land?"

**Person:** "Yeah, I see that too."

**You:** "Okay. So going forward, you'll [new approach]. And I'll be here if you need support. Does that make sense?"

**Person:** "Yes."

**You:** "Good. This is how we all learn. You tried, it didn't go perfectly, you learned. That's exactly what I want."

## Chapter 16: Reframing Feedback — From Punishment to Growth

### The Problem With How Most Managers Give Feedback

Most managers are terrible at giving feedback.

They give it rarely (because it's uncomfortable). When they do give it, it's often in a moment of frustration (which sounds like criticism). And they give it in a way that puts people on the defensive.

Result: People hear feedback as punishment. They get defensive. They don't change. And the manager feels like feedback doesn't work.

But feedback doesn't fail. The *way* you give feedback fails.

### The Concept: Feedback Is a Gift, Not a Judgment

Here's the shift that changes everything:

**Don't think:** "I need to criticize them so they improve."

**Think:** "I care about their growth. I'm going to give them information they can use to get better."

Feedback isn't judgment. It's data. It's a gift to help them improve.

When people receive feedback as "I care about your growth," they respond differently than when they receive it as "I'm criticizing you."

### Why Managers Get Feedback Wrong

**Reason 1: They Give It in Anger**

"You made a mistake and I'm frustrated. So I give feedback right now, harshly."

Result: The person hears attack, not growth opportunity.

**Reason 2: They Frame It as Judgment**

"You did this wrong. That's not how a good [person/manager/team member] does it."

Result: Personal attack, not specific feedback.

**Reason 3: They Don't Follow Up**

"You need to improve on X." Then they never mention it again.

Result: The person doesn't know if they improved. They don't know it's important. So they don't change.

**Reason 4: They Focus Only on What's Wrong**

"Here's everything you're doing wrong."

Result: Demoralizing. Person doesn't know what they should keep doing. They get defensive.

**Reason 5: They Don't Give Enough Feedback**

"I'll give them feedback at review time, once a year."

Result: Feedback is delayed. It's not actionable. Change is slow.

### What Good Feedback Looks Like

Good feedback:
- **Is specific** ("You interrupted the customer three times" vs. "You don't listen")
- **Is timely** (soon after the behavior, while it matters)
- **Is balanced** (here's what you did well AND here's what to work on)
- **Focuses on behavior, not character** ("When you came late to meetings" vs. "You're unreliable")
- **Connects to impact** ("When you do X, it affects Y")
- **Offers help** ("Here's how you can improve. I'm here to support.")
- **Is frequent** (not waiting for formal reviews)

### The Feedback Framework

**Step 1: Set Up Well**

Find a private moment. "Can we talk for 10 minutes? I want to give you some feedback."

This is not ambush feedback. It's intentional.

**Step 2: Start With Context**

"I want to talk about what happened in the customer call yesterday. I noticed something I think would be useful for you to know."

Be calm and factual.

**Step 3: Describe the Behavior (Not the Person)**

"You interrupted the customer four times. Each time they were explaining their concern, you jumped in with a solution."

Specific. Observable. Not "You don't listen."

**Step 4: Explain the Impact**

"When you do that, the customer doesn't feel heard. They get frustrated. That makes it harder to build trust."

Connect behavior to outcome.

**Step 5: Add Context (What They Did Well)**

"And I also noticed you stayed calm under pressure. That's great. You kept trying to problem-solve. I appreciate that."

Not all negative. Show what's working.

**Step 6: Ask For Their Perspective**

"What was going on for you in that call? Help me understand your thinking."

Maybe they had reasons you don't know. Listen.

**Step 7: Partner on Improvement**

"Here's what I think would help: Listen to the full concern before offering solutions. I know that's hard sometimes, but it builds trust faster. Next time, I'll listen in and we can debrief. Does that work?"

Not: "Here's what you need to do."

Rather: "Here's what would help. How can we make this happen?"

**Step 8: Follow Up**

A week later: "How's it going with listening more before jumping to solutions? What are you noticing?"

Make it ongoing, not one-time.

### Real Scenario: The Feedback That Changed Performance

Meera was Rahul's manager. Rahul was technically strong but abrupt with customers. He was good at closing deals but customers didn't like him.

Meera noticed it but didn't say anything for months. She hoped he'd figure it out.

Then she got feedback from a customer: "Rahul is smart, but he's rude. We don't want to work with him again."

That was the wake-up call. Meera decided to give feedback.

**The Old Way (What Meera Almost Did):**

"Rahul, you need to work on your customer relationships. You're too abrupt. Customers don't like you. If this doesn't improve, you're going to have a real problem."

**Rahul's Response:** Defensive. "I'm just being direct. That's who I am."

**What Actually Happened:**

Meera gave feedback differently.

"I want to talk about something I've noticed. When you're solving a customer's problem, you jump straight to the solution. You don't take time to understand their concern. I saw this with [specific customer]. You came up with three solutions before they finished explaining."

Rahul: "I was trying to help quickly."

Meera: "I get it. And I appreciate that you want to solve things fast. Here's what I've noticed: When you do that, customers feel rushed. They feel like you don't really care about their problem, just about closing the deal. Even if that's not what you mean, that's how it lands."

Rahul: "Oh. I didn't realize that."

Meera: "I know. So here's what might help: Ask one more clarifying question before you propose a solution. Let them feel heard. It'll actually lead to better solutions because you'll understand the problem better. And it'll build loyalty."

Rahul: "I can try that."

Meera: "Great. Let's do some role-plays so you can practice. I'll give you customer scenarios and you show me how you'd approach them."

Over the next month, Meera gave frequent feedback (every few days when Rahul was practicing, then weekly).

"That was great—you really listened to that customer's concern before offering solutions."

"Remember that thing about asking clarifying questions? You nailed it in that call."

After three months: Rahul's customer satisfaction scores went up. He was still direct, but people felt heard.

And Rahul was grateful for the feedback. "I wouldn't have changed if you hadn't told me specifically what the problem was and how it affected customers."

### Conversation Script: Giving Feedback Well

---

**You:** "I want to give you some feedback. Is now a good time?"

**Person:** "Sure."

**You:** "Thanks. So in the call with [customer] yesterday, I noticed you had some strong reactions when they said they wanted to delay the application. You said 'That's not a good idea' pretty firmly. Help me understand what was going on."

**Person:** "They were making a mistake. I wanted to tell them."

**You:** "I get it. I appreciate that you're trying to help. Here's what I noticed: When you tell customers they're wrong directly, they get defensive. They dig in more. What might work better: Try 'I understand. Can I share what usually happens when applications get delayed?' That way, you're helping them see the issue without making them wrong."

**Person:** "That makes sense."

**You:** "I also want to say: You do follow through well with customers. You make sure they understand the process. That's a strength. I just want to help you pair that with more patience when they're resisting. Does that make sense?"

**Person:** "Yeah. I'll try that."

**You:** "Good. Let's role-play it a few times so you can feel how it lands. And I'll listen in on your next few calls and give you quick feedback. Sound good?"

**Person:** "Okay."
