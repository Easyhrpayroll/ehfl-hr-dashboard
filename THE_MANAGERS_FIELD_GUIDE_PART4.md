## Chapter 8: Why Hiring Is Your Most Important Decision

### The Single Biggest Impact on Your Team's Performance

Here's something that takes most managers years to realize: **Who you hire determines everything else.**

You can have all the management skills in the world. You can be brilliant at motivation, feedback, accountability. But if you hire the wrong people, you'll be spending 80% of your energy fixing hiring mistakes.

Conversely, if you hire well, your job becomes easier. Good people don't need constant supervision. They self-motivate. They solve problems. They make you look good.

This is why hiring is the most important decision you'll make as a manager.

And it's also where most managers fail.

### The Concept: Hiring Is Harder Than It Looks

Here's what most managers do when hiring:

1. They get a resume
2. They have a conversation
3. They like the person
4. They hire them

That's it. 30 minutes to an hour of evaluation to make a decision that will affect your team for the next two years.

And then they're surprised when the person doesn't work out.

Good hiring requires rigorous thinking. It requires clarity on what you actually need. It requires tough interviewing. It requires being honest about whether someone is a fit—even if they're a nice person.

### Why Managers Fail at Hiring

**Reason 1: Unclear on What You Need**

Most job descriptions are generic. "We need a relationship manager. 3+ years experience, strong communication, results-oriented."

That could describe 500 people. And it doesn't tell you what the role actually requires.

Result: You can't evaluate candidates against requirements because you don't know what the requirements are.

**Reason 2: Overweighting Likeability**

You meet someone. They're charming. They're good at interviews. You like them.

So you hire them.

But likeability in an interview is a skill. It doesn't predict job performance.

Result: You hire people who interview well but can't do the job.

**Reason 3: Confirmation Bias**

You like someone. So you interpret everything in their favor.

They say "I left my last job because I wanted growth." You think: "Great, ambitious."

But what they might mean: "I couldn't get along with my manager and blamed them."

Result: You hire someone and miss red flags that were there all along.

**Reason 4: Rushing**

You have open roles. You're short-staffed. You need someone now.

So you hire the first adequate person who walks in.

Result: You make a hiring mistake that will haunt you for two years.

**Reason 5: Not Checking References**

You say you'll call references. Then you don't. Too busy.

Result: You miss crucial information from people who actually know how the person works.

### What Good Managers Do

Good managers treat hiring like the serious decision it is.

They:
- Spend time understanding what the role actually needs
- Create a rigorous interview process
- Interview multiple candidates
- Check references thoroughly
- Make hiring decisions based on capability and fit, not likeability
- Say no to good people who aren't right fits
- Invest time in onboarding great hires

Good managers also know: **Making a bad hire is easy. Fixing a bad hire is hard.**

It's faster to hire slowly and carefully than to hire quickly and fix mistakes.

### The Cost of Bad Hiring

Let's do the math:

**Bad hire costs:**
- Salary and benefits for person who doesn't perform: $4,00,000
- Recruiting and onboarding time wasted: $20,000 (your time)
- Productivity hit to your team (broken morale, knowledge gaps, extra load): $50,000
- Time to exit bad hire: $10,000 (legal, severance, knowledge transfer)
- Time to recruit replacement: $20,000

Total: ~$5,00,000 loss for one bad hire.

**Good hiring investment:**
- Extra recruiting time: $5,000
- Rigorous interviewing: $3,000
- Reference checks: $1,000

Total: ~$9,000 investment

If you avoid one bad hire every two years through better hiring, you've saved $2,55,000 in the time it takes to do this job well.

Hiring well is not a luxury. It's the most profitable thing you can do.

### What Bad Managers Do

**Bad Manager:** "I need someone now. This person has the right experience. Close enough. I'll hire them."

**Result:** 6 months later, you realize the person can't do the job. Or they can but they're slow. Or they don't fit the culture. Now you have to manage them out or live with mediocre performance.

**Bad Manager:** "I like this person. Even though they don't have the right background, we'll make it work."

**Result:** You spend two years trying to teach them things that should have been baseline capability. Meanwhile, your high performers are frustrated because they're carrying extra weight.

### Real Scenario: The Expensive Bad Hire

Rahul was a lending manager. He had two roles open. He interviewed three candidates. His favorite candidate, Anand, was charming and impressive. "I've always delivered results," Anand said confidently.

Rahul hired Anand based on the interview and the resume.

Three months later, Anand was underperforming. His loan approval rates were low. Customer satisfaction was low. He wasn't following process. When Rahul gave feedback, Anand got defensive. "This is just how I work."

Rahul invested time trying to coach Anand. It didn't work. At six months, Rahul realized: This isn't going to work. We need to part ways.

By that point, Anand had damaged relationships with customers. He'd slowed down the team (others had to pick up his load). He'd created tension (his defensiveness made coaching difficult).

Rahul had to exit Anand. It took another two months to do cleanly.

By month eight, Rahul was recruiting again.

Meanwhile, Rahul's other candidate, Priya, turned out to be perfect. She was quiet in the interview. Less impressive to watch. But she was methodical, asked good questions, fit the culture perfectly. She's been one of the best hires Rahul ever made.

The lesson: The person who impresses in an interview isn't always the person who will perform in the job.

### What Hiring Well Looks Like

We'll go deep into this in the next three chapters, but here's the preview:

**Step 1: Get Clear on the Role**
Define what you actually need. Not a generic job description. Specific requirements. What will success look like in 30 days? 6 months? 1 year?

**Step 2: Design the Job**
Use the ASK framework: Accountability, Skills, Knowledge. What are they accountable for? What skills do they need? What knowledge must they have?

**Step 3: Rigorous Interviewing**
Ask questions that reveal how they actually work. Don't just listen to their story. Probe. Ask for examples. See if the story holds up.

**Step 4: Check References**
Call people who know how they work. Ask specific questions. Get honest feedback.

**Step 5: Make the Decision**
Based on capability and fit. Not likeability. Not impressive stories. Real capability in the role.

### Conversation Script: The "No" Conversation

This is hard, but it's important:

---

**You (to candidate):** "Thank you for interviewing with us. I want to be direct: We're not going to move forward."

**Candidate:** "Can I ask why?"

**You:** "We were looking for someone with specific experience in [area]. You have great experience in [different area]. That experience is valuable somewhere, but it's not what we need for this specific role."

**Candidate:** "Okay."

**You:** "I want to be honest so you can make good choices about roles you apply for. Does that make sense?"

---

This is harder than hiring someone you like but isn't right. But it's the right call.

- Primary outcome: [e.g., close 40 loans/month]
- Secondary outcomes: [e.g., maintain 95%+ customer satisfaction, develop junior team members]

**Skills (What must they be able to do?)**
- Must-have skill 1: [e.g., objection handling]
- Must-have skill 2: [e.g., relationship building]
- Must-have skill 3: [e.g., process discipline]
- Nice-to-have skill: [e.g., experience with specific product]

**Knowledge (What must they know?)**
- Must know: [e.g., NBFC lending process]
- Must know: [e.g., regulatory framework]
- Nice to know: [e.g., local market dynamics]

- Work style: [e.g., self-motivated, collaborative]
- Values alignment: [e.g., customer focus, integrity]
- Energy level: [e.g., high pace, detail-oriented]

Now, when you interview, you evaluate candidates against this matrix. Not against gut feel.

### Quick Reference Checklist

Before hiring someone:

- [ ] Are you clear on what the role actually requires?
- [ ] Have you interviewed at least 3-5 candidates?
- [ ] Have you asked behavioral questions (how they actually work, not how they think)?
- [ ] Have you checked references?
- [ ] Can you say yes to: "This person can do the job well AND fits our culture"?

If you can't say yes to all of these, keep looking.

### Reflection Questions

1. **Think about your last hire.** Did you take the time to be rigorous? Or did you hire quickly because you were short-staffed?

2. **Think about your best hire.** What made that person work out? What did you do right?

3. **Think about your worst hire.** What did you miss? What would you do differently?

4. **How much time do you usually spend interviewing candidates?** Is it enough?

5. **What's one hiring mistake you're afraid of making?** How can you prevent it?

If you don't have an open role, review your current team. Who's performing? Who isn't? For the people who aren't, ask: "Is this a capability issue that training can fix? Or was this a hiring mistake?"

For hiring mistakes, what are you going to do?

## Chapter 9: Job Design — The ASK Framework

### The Problem Most Managers Don't See

You post a job description. You get 50 applicants. You interview 5. You hire 1.

Three months later, you're disappointed. The person isn't doing well.

You blame the hire: "They're not capable."

But what if the problem isn't the hire? What if the problem is that you never clearly defined what the job actually is?

Most job descriptions are vague. "We're looking for a motivated person who can drive results."

That could mean anything. Different candidates interpret it different ways. They come in with different expectations. Then the gap between expectation and reality becomes the problem.

Good job design is how you prevent this.

### The Concept: ASK Framework

**A = Accountability**

What are they responsible for? What outcomes matter? What will you measure?

This isn't general. It's specific. "Drive sales" is not specific. "Close 40 loans per month with 95%+ customer satisfaction" is specific.

**S = Skills**

What can they do? What abilities do they need to have? What would you see them do well?

Examples: "Can handle objections without getting defensive." "Can build rapport with customers quickly." "Can work through problems systematically."

**K = Knowledge**

What do they need to know? What's the baseline knowledge for this job?

Examples: "Knows NBFC lending guidelines." "Understands the underwriting process." "Knows how to use the CRM system."

### Why This Matters

When you define a job through ASK, two things happen:

**1. You're clear on what you need.**

You can't hire for something you haven't defined. ASK forces you to get specific.

**2. Candidates know what they're signing up for.**

No surprises. No gaps between expectation and reality. They know: "This role requires X, Y, and Z. I either have it or I don't."

### Accountability: Getting Specific

This is the hardest part. But it's also the most important.

Bad accountability: "Drive team performance."

Good accountability:
- Close 40 loans per month (with quality: 95%+ customer satisfaction)
- Maintain portfolio health (< 5% overdue accounts)
- Develop one junior team member

Now, when you interview, you can ask: "Can you consistently close 40 quality loans per month?" And you can evaluate their answer.

**How to get specific:**

Start with the business outcome. "We need $400 crore in loan approvals this month."

Translate to team level. "That's $10 crore per team member. So we need 40 loans per person."

Translate to individual outcomes. "Your accountability is 40 loans per month with high customer satisfaction."

Also include what success looks like in terms of behavior and capability: "You'll achieve this by building strong customer relationships, handling objections effectively, and working systematically through your pipeline."

Now a candidate can answer: "Yes, I can do that" or "No, that's outside my range."

### Skills: What They Must Be Able to Do

Skills are things they can demonstrate. They can do them, or they can't (yet).

**Financial industry skills:**
- Objection handling (can they handle customer concerns without getting defensive?)
- Relationship building (can they build trust quickly?)
- Problem-solving (can they work through complex situations?)
- Attention to detail (can they follow process without cutting corners?)
- Persistence (can they handle rejection and keep going?)

**How to identify required skills:**

Watch your best performers. What do they do that your average performers don't? Those are the critical skills.

Ask your top performers: "What do you do well that makes you successful in this role?"

Watch your underperformers. What are they weak at? Those gaps are the skills you need to screen for.

**How to assess skills in interviews:**

Ask behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time you handled an angry customer. What did you do?"

Listen to their approach. Do they take responsibility? Do they problem-solve? Or do they defend themselves?

Ask follow-ups: "What would you do differently?" "How did the customer respond?"

Probe deep. One story isn't enough. Ask for three examples and see if there's a pattern.

### Knowledge: What They Must Know

Knowledge is information they need to have. Either they know it, or they can be trained.

**But here's the important distinction:** Some knowledge can be trained quickly. Some takes months.

**Knowledge you should screen for (takes months to learn):**
- NBFC lending process
- Regulatory requirements
- Product knowledge
- Customer types in your market

**Knowledge you can train (takes weeks to learn):**
- Your specific company's process
- Your specific CRM system
- Your specific underwriting guidelines
- Your compensation model

When you hire, focus on must-have knowledge. Screen for it. If they don't have it, you'll be training them for months.

Nice-to-have knowledge is a bonus, but not a dealbreaker.

**How to assess knowledge:**

Ask directly: "Describe your understanding of the NBFC lending process."

Listen to their answer. Do they understand the basics? Can they explain it clearly?

Ask scenarios: "A customer has a good income but recently filed for bankruptcy. What does that mean for their application?"

If they can't answer, ask: "Are you willing to learn this?"

If yes, it's learnable. If they get defensive or dismissive, it's a problem.

### Real Scenario: The Job Design that Changed Everything

Meera was hiring a collections specialist. Her old hire had quit. She was frustrated: "Nobody wants to do this job. It's stressful. It's thankless."

She posted a generic job description: "We need a motivated collections professional. You'll be collecting on overdue accounts. Experience required."

She got applicants who sounded good. But when she hired them, they quickly realized: The job is stressful. Customers are angry. The outcomes are hard to achieve. They quit.

Then Meera learned about ASK. She redesigned the job:

**Accountability:**
- Recover 75% of assigned portfolio value
- Maintain ethical standards (no threats, no harassment)
- Complete all required documentation
- Mentor one junior collector

**Skills:**
- Emotional regulation (can stay calm under stress)
- Empathy (can understand customer problems, not just demand payment)
- Problem-solving (can find creative solutions—payment plans, asset sales, etc.)
- Persistence (can handle repeated rejection)

**Knowledge:**
- Debt collection laws and regulations
- NBFC's policies
- Customer database systems
- Collections strategies

Now, when Meera interviewed, she could ask:

"Collections is stressful. Customers get angry. Tell me about a time you handled an angry person. How did you stay calm? What did you do?"

She could assess: Can this person actually handle the stress? Or will they quit in three months?

"Recovery rates are tough to hit. Tell me about a time you had to achieve a hard target. How did you do it? What obstacles did you face?"

This told her if they had the persistence and problem-solving to actually recover 75%.

Her next three hires worked out much better. Not because the candidates were different. But because she was clearer on what the job required and she was assessing against those requirements.

---

**[You explain Accountability]**

**"Can you consistently do that?"**

**[Listen to their answer. Probe if they seem uncertain.]**

**"To do this well, you need to be good at relationship building, problem-solving, and persistence. You'll face rejection regularly. You need to handle that without getting discouraged."**

**[Listen and probe.]**

**[You explain Knowledge]**

**"You need to understand NBFC lending—how approvals work, what disqualifies an applicant, the regulatory requirements. Do you have that background?"**

**"If not, are you willing to spend the first two months learning?"**

**[Listen to willingness to learn.]**

## Chapter 10: Interview Techniques — How to Hire the Right Person

### The Truth About Interviews

Most managers think interviews are conversations. You have a nice chat. If you like the person, you hire them.

But interviews are actually auditions.

The candidate is performing. They're showing you their best self. They're answering the questions in the way they think will get the job.

Your job is to see past the performance. To see how they actually work.

This requires different skills than just conversation. It requires interrogation (in a professional way). It requires listening for what they're *not* saying. It requires asking follow-up questions.

### The Concept: Behavioral Interviewing

The best predictor of how someone will work in the future is how they've worked in the past.

That means:

- Don't ask: "How would you handle an angry customer?"
- Do ask: "Tell me about a time you handled an angry customer. What did you do?"

The first question gets their imaginary answer. The second gets their actual behavior.

Behavioral interviewing is asking for real examples and digging into them.

### The Four Key Principles

**Principle 1: Ask Open-Ended Questions**

Bad: "Are you good with people?"

Good: "Tell me about a time you had to build a strong relationship quickly. What did you do? How did it work out?"

**Principle 2: Listen for Specifics**

When they tell a story, listen for details.

"I handled an angry customer" is vague.

"The customer was upset because their application was delayed. They'd waited two weeks. I apologized for the delay, explained why it was happening, and gave them a timeline for approval" is specific.

Specifics are credible. Generalities are suspect.

**Principle 3: Ask Follow-Up Questions**

When they finish a story, ask:

- "What would you do differently?"
- "Why did you choose that approach?"
- "What was the outcome?"
- "What did you learn?"
- "Has this changed how you approach similar situations?"

Most candidates give you their rehearsed story. Follow-ups make them think. That's when you see who they really are.

**Principle 4: Listen to How They Take Feedback**

This is a secret weapon most managers don't use.

In the interview, gently challenge something they said: "Interesting. When I heard that, I wondered... Did you consider...?"

Watch their response.

Do they get defensive? Do they blame others? Or do they think about it? Do they acknowledge the point? Do they give you new information?

How they take feedback in an interview is how they'll take feedback on the job.

### The Interview Framework: Structured Approach

Prepare five topic areas. For each area, prepare 2-3 behavioral questions.

**Topic 1: Handling Difficulty**
- Tell me about a time you had to do something challenging. What made it hard? How did you handle it?
- Tell me about a time you failed at something. What happened? What did you learn?

**Topic 2: Relationship Building**
- Tell me about a time you built a strong relationship with a customer (or colleague). How did you do it?
- Tell me about a time someone was difficult to get along with. How did you handle it?

**Topic 3: Work Standards**
- Tell me about a time you had to maintain quality when you were under pressure. What happened?
- Tell me about a time you didn't meet a commitment. Why? What did you do?

**Topic 4: Problem-Solving**
- Tell me about a time you solved a problem. How did you approach it?
- Tell me about a time you were stuck on something. How did you get unstuck?

**Topic 5: Motivation and Drive**
- What are you looking for in your next role?
- Tell me about a time you went above and beyond. Why?

**For each answer:**

- Listen to the story
- Ask 2-3 follow-up questions
- Listen to how they describe their role (Did they own it? Blame others? Give credit?)
- Notice their emotion (Do they seem proud? Defensive? Reflective?)

### What To Listen For

**Red Flags:**

"My manager didn't understand me." (Blame shifting)

"I had to do everything because my team wasn't capable." (Doesn't develop people)

"I couldn't get the job done because of company politics." (Blame)

"I left because I wasn't valued." (Victim mentality)

"My team didn't follow my direction." (Autocratic)

**Green Flags:**

"I made a mistake. Here's what I learned." (Takes responsibility)

"My manager didn't trust me initially, so I had to prove myself." (Understands he has to earn trust)

"When I struggled, I asked for help." (Coachable)

"I realized I was micromanaging, so I backed off." (Self-aware)

"The team solved it better than I would have." (Develops people)

### Real Scenario: The Interview That Revealed the Truth

Aditya was hiring for a relationship manager role. He interviewed three candidates. All had good resumes. All had relevant experience.

Candidate 1, Rajesh, told a great story: "At my last job, I increased sales by 40%. I did this by implementing a new process, training my team, and holding them accountable. I transformed the team."

Aditya was impressed. So he dug deeper:

"Tell me specifically what process you implemented."

Rajesh: "I had the team track all their activities. I reviewed their calls. I told them exactly how to talk to customers."

"How did your team respond?"

"Initially, they resisted. But once they saw results, they came around."

More probing: "Tell me about someone on your team who didn't come around."

Rajesh: "We had to exit one person because they couldn't adapt."

Red flags started appearing. Rajesh's "transformation" sounded like micromanagement. When Aditya asked about feedback he'd received, Rajesh got defensive. "My manager wanted me to be more collaborative, but I got results."

**Candidate 2, Priya,** told this story: "My sales were solid, but I realized I was doing most of the work. My team wasn't developing. I decided to step back and coach instead of direct. It was uncomfortable because results dipped initially. But over six months, my team's capability increased. My backup grew confident enough to handle accounts. I got promoted partly because of that team strength."

When probed: "What made you realize you were limiting your team?"

"My manager gave me feedback that I was too hands-on. I fought it initially, then realized she was right. It was humbling."

Green flag: She took feedback.

"What's one thing you'd do differently?"

"I'd delegate more confidently from day one instead of waiting for feedback."

This candidate was self-aware, coachable, and developed people.

Aditya hired Priya. Not because Rajesh's sales were lower. But because Priya would actually develop people and build capability, while Rajesh would burn people out.

### The Right Way to Structure an Interview

**Before the Interview:**
- Prepare your five topic areas
- Prepare specific behavioral questions
- Review their resume and note anything you want to explore
- Have a panel if possible (more eyes, more data)

**During the Interview:**

**5 minutes:** Build rapport. "Tell me about your background."

**30 minutes:** Deep dive into behavioral questions. Ask about handling difficulty, relationships, work standards, problem-solving, drive.

**5 minutes:** Let them ask questions.

**5 minutes:** Tell them about the role and next steps. "If we move forward, here's what happens next."

**After the Interview:**
- Debrief with your panel (if you had one)
- Note your observations
- Rate them on each of your key criteria
- Compare across candidates
- Check references for top candidates

### What Bad Interviewers Do

**Bad:** "That's great. I like you. Let's hire you."

This based on likeability, not capability.

**Bad:** "Tell me about yourself." [Listens to resume summary] "Great. Any questions?"

No probing. No depth. Just surface.

**Bad:** They ask softball questions that anyone could answer well: "What are your strengths?" "Where do you want to be in five years?"

These questions get rehearsed answers. They don't reveal much.

**Bad:** They interview people the same way. No customization to the role.

**Bad:** They don't check references because they're busy or because they "trust their gut."

Your gut is wrong 40% of the time. References are right 90% of the time.

### Conversation Script: The Reference Check Call

This is crucial but often skipped:

---

**You:** "Hi, I'm calling because [Candidate Name] applied for a role with us. I wanted to understand more about how they work. Do you have 10 minutes?"

**Reference:** "Sure."

**You:** "What was [Candidate Name]'s role when you knew them?"

**Reference:** [Answers]

**You:** "What were they best at?"

**Reference:** [Answers]

**You:** "What were they less good at? Where did they struggle?"

**Reference:** [Answers - this is the critical question]

**You:** "If you were in my shoes, would you hire them? Why or why not?"

**Reference:** [Honest assessment]

**You:** "Is there anything you'd want me to know that I haven't asked?"

**Reference:** [Often reveals something important]

---

The key question is the third one. Most people will say good things. But "Where did they struggle?" gets real answers.

## Chapter 11: Making the Hire Decision

### The Moment of Truth

You've done the work. You've defined the role with ASK. You've interviewed carefully. You've checked references.

Now comes the hardest part: Making the decision.

Because at this point, you usually have one candidate you really like and one or two others who are adequate.

Your instinct is: Hire the one you like.

But that's exactly when you need to step back and think clearly.

### The Concept: Decide Based on Capability and Fit, Not Preference

**Preference** = I like this person. They interview well. I'd enjoy working with them.

**Capability** = They can actually do this job well.

**Fit** = They align with our values and way of working.

These are different things.

You can like someone and not hire them if they can't do the job.

You can hire someone and not particularly like them if they can do the job well.

The hardest decision is when you like someone but they're not quite right. Should you hire them anyway?

The answer is almost always no.

### Why Managers Compromise on Hiring Decisions

**Reason 1: Impatience**

"We've been looking for three months. This person is good enough. Let's hire them."

But hiring someone who's "good enough" in the wrong way costs more than waiting two more months for the right person.

**Reason 2: Likeability**

"I really like this person. And they're close on capability. Surely we can develop them into the role."

Maybe. But capability gaps take a long time to close. And people's fundamental approach to work rarely changes.

**Reason 3: The Resume**

"Their resume is perfect. They must be right."

Resumes are curated. They show what people want to show. They don't show how people actually work.

**Reason 4: Fear of Saying No**

"All three candidates are decent. I'm going to hire the one I like best."

What if one of them is actually better than the others on your key criteria? You'll never know if you just pick the one you like.

### The Decision Matrix: Objective Hiring

Instead of gut feel, use a matrix.

List your top 5-7 criteria (from your ASK framework):

1. Can handle objections without defensiveness
2. Follows process consistently
3. Builds relationships with customers
4. Solves problems systematically
5. Takes responsibility (doesn't blame)
6. Coachable (takes feedback well)
7. Fits our Results + Relationships culture

Now, rate each candidate on each criterion (1 = Weak, 5 = Strong):

| Criterion | Rajesh | Priya | Arun |
|-----------|--------|-------|------|
| Objection handling | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Process discipline | 3 | 5 | 4 |
| Relationship building | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Problem-solving | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Takes responsibility | 2 | 5 | 4 |
| Coachable | 2 | 5 | 4 |
| Culture fit | 3 | 5 | 4 |
| **Total** | **22** | **35** | **25** |

Now you can see clearly: Priya is ahead in most areas. She's stronger on the behavioral items (takes responsibility, coachable) that predict long-term success.

Rajesh has solid technical capability but weak on personal accountability and coachability. That's a red flag for long-term performance.

Arun is solid across the board but not exceptional anywhere.

The decision becomes clear: Hire Priya.

### What Good Hiring Managers Do

Good managers:
- Use a decision matrix to objectively compare
- Hire based on capability and fit, not preference
- Are willing to say no to decent candidates if they're not right
- Understand that hiring slowly is better than hiring quickly
- Check references thoroughly
- Have a clear onboarding plan for the person they hire

Good managers also:
- Admit when they've hired someone weak and act quickly
- Learn from hiring mistakes
- Improve their hiring process over time

### What Bad Hiring Managers Do

**Bad:** "I like this person. I'm going to hire them even though they're not quite right."

Result: Bad hire that haunts you.

**Bad:** "They'll learn on the job. We can develop them."

Result: You spend two years trying to teach them things they should know.

**Bad:** "This is the best of the three, so I'll hire them."

Result: You hire someone who's only good relative to a weak candidate pool.

**Bad:** "I can't believe I made this mistake. Why didn't I see it in the interview?"

Result: No learning. Same mistake again.

### Real Scenario: The Hire That Almost Went Wrong

Vikram was hiring a collections specialist. He'd interviewed three candidates:

- Candidate A was charming, answered questions well, had a similar background to Vikram.
- Candidate B was quiet, asked clarifying questions, admitted when she didn't know something.
- Candidate C was okay. Middle of the road.

Vikram's gut said: Hire A. They'd get along great.

But he'd been through a bad hire before. So he used a decision matrix.

On "Handles Pressure Without Getting Defensive," Candidate A scored 2. She got defensive when Vikram gently challenged her approach.

Candidate B scored 5. When challenged, she said: "That's a good point. I hadn't thought of it that way. Let me reconsider."

On "Takes Responsibility" (important in collections), Candidate A's examples involved people not listening to her. Candidate B's examples involved mistakes she made and what she learned.

Score: A = 2, B = 5.

Vikram's matrix showed: B was stronger on the behavioral/character factors that predict long-term success. A was more charming in the interview.

Vikram hired B.

Six months later, B was performing well. Collections are stressful. There was a month where recovery was low. Vikram gave her tough feedback.

B responded: "That's hard to hear. Let me look at what I'm missing. I think I'm being too soft on restructuring—I should be more assertive."

She got better.

Vikram realized: If he'd hired A, and given her the same feedback, he probably would have gotten defensiveness and blame-shifting.

The "boring" candidate was the right hire.

### The Onboarding Plan: Making Your Hire Successful

Once you make the decision, you're not done. Now you have to set them up to succeed.

**Week 1:**
- First day orientation (company, policies, team)
- Role clarity (here's what you're accountable for)
- Meet key stakeholders
- Understand the systems

**Week 2-4:**
- Shadow current top performer
- Observe the real work
- Start practicing with supervision
- Daily check-ins

**Month 2-3:**
- Gradually increase autonomy
- Weekly coaching on performance and learning
- Introduce them to customers/stakeholders
- Get feedback from others on how they're doing

**Month 3+:**
- Independent performance
- Weekly check-ins (at least monthly after that)
- Feedback on both performance and growth

**The critical thing:** Your involvement in the first three months determines the success of the hire.

New people need structure and support. Provide it, and they'll come up to speed in 90 days. Skip it, and you'll spend two years wondering why they're not better.

### Conversation Script: The Offer and Onboarding Conversation

---

**You:** "We'd like to make you an offer. You'd start in two weeks. Here's what the first week looks like: [explain onboarding]. Here's what success looks like after 30 days: [explain expectations]. And here's how I'll support you: [explain coaching, check-ins, etc.]"

**Candidate:** [Accepts or negotiates]

**You:** "Great. I'm excited to have you on the team. My goal is for you to be successful. I'll be very hands-on the first few months. We'll have daily check-ins. You'll watch our top performer. You'll get feedback constantly. My role is to make sure you succeed."

**Candidate:** "Sounds good."

**You:** "Welcome to the team."
